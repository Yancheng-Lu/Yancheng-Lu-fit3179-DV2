01

A Cinema in Motion

The number of Australian films represented in the dataset changes considerably from year to year.

From a Quiet Beginning to a Growing Presence

Australian cinema did not grow overnight. Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, Australian-linked films appeared only sporadically in the dataset, with most years recording fewer than five titles. The early 2000s brought signs of growth, but the real turning point came in 2010, when the recorded number of films rose sharply.

By 2014, production representation reached its peak of 31 films, marking a striking contrast with earlier decades. Yet growth was far from steady. The following years saw repeated rises and falls, suggesting a changing rather than consistently expanding film landscape.

02

Genres Rise, Fall and Return

Genre rankings reveal how different types of Australian films moved in and out of prominence.

The Changing Faces of Australian Cinema

Australian cinema has never been defined by a single genre. Comedy dominated the 1980s and 1990s, ranking first in both decades. However, the spotlight gradually shifted as Action claimed the top position in the 2000s, followed by Drama in the 2010s.

By the 2020s, Horror had climbed to first place, while Comedy had fallen to fifth, marking a striking reversal in the genre rankings.

Yet not every genre followed this cycle. Drama remained remarkably consistent, ranking among the top three across all five decades. Meanwhile, Animation returned to the top five in the 2020s.

These shifting rankings reveal how the genre landscape represented in the dataset has evolved, with different types of films rising to prominence over time.

03

Changing Genres, Changing Fortunes

Median box-office gross shows that commercial fortunes differed across both genre and era.

More Films, Not Always Bigger Fortunes

A genre’s prominence does not always translate into box-office success. The 2000s stood out as a period of remarkable commercial performance in the dataset, with Animation reaching a median gross of $260 million, followed by Horror at $68.3 million and Comedy at $55 million.

Yet these fortunes did not last. By the 2020s, Animation's median had fallen to $1.2 million, while Comedy dropped below $1 million.

The most striking contrast comes from Horror. Although it ranked first in film count during the 2020s, its median gross was just $1.3 million, compared with $7 million for Action.

Together, these patterns reveal an important distinction: producing more films does not necessarily mean earning more at the box office.

Commercial Success

What does success look like?

Box office, production budgets and ratings tell different stories about how Australian films performed.

04

From Era to Genre to Box Office

Follow films from their era, through genre, and into different box-office tiers.

Many Films, Few Blockbusters

As Australian cinema evolved, its journey to box-office success followed very different paths. The 2010s contributed the largest flow of films with reported box-office earnings, while Action emerged as one of the most prominent individual genres.

Yet more films did not necessarily mean more blockbusters. Despite the diversity of genres and decades, the largest share of films flowed into the Under $10 million category.

Action films appeared across all box-office tiers, including the highest, but only a relatively small proportion of films reached $150 million or more.

This reveals a striking contrast: while Australian-linked cinema expanded its presence in the dataset, major commercial breakthroughs remained comparatively rare.

05

Blockbusters and Expensive Misses

Reported production budgets and box-office gross do not always move together.

Big Budgets, Bigger Risks

In cinema, spending millions does not guarantee making millions. While some Australian-linked films achieved extraordinary box-office returns, others struggled to recover even their reported production budgets.

The Matrix Reloaded stands out as a major success, with its worldwide gross exceeding its production budget by $588.6 million. Similarly, The Lego Movie and The Matrix recorded impressive gaps of over $400 million.

Yet the opposite story is just as striking. The Adventures of Pluto Nash earned approximately $92.9 million less than its production budget, followed by Better Man with a gap of $87.5 million.

These contrasting outcomes reveal a fundamental reality of filmmaking: a larger investment may create greater opportunities, but it also brings substantial financial risk. Even ambitious productions can fall far short of commercial expectations.

06

Did Audiences Think the Films Got Better?

Compare the share of low, moderate and highly rated films across decades.

More Films, But Were They Better?

As Australian-linked cinema expanded, did audience ratings improve alongside it? Surprisingly, the answer is far from straightforward.

The 1990s emerged as the strongest decade for highly rated films, with 37.1% scoring 7 or above. Yet this positive picture changed dramatically in the 2000s, when the share of highly rated films dropped to just 12.5%, while low-rated films rose to 45.3%.

By the 2020s, the balance had shifted again. Low-rated films declined to 20.8%, but a striking 60% received moderate ratings, suggesting a move towards more consistently average reception rather than exceptional acclaim.

These shifts reveal that more films do not necessarily mean better-rated films. Explore different genres to see whether this pattern holds across Australian cinema.

07

The Companies Behind Australian Film

Production companies differ in both output and audience reception.

Behind the Screen: A Few Names, Many Films

Behind every film lies a network of companies, but not all have an equal presence in Australian-linked cinema.

Warner Bros. dominates the dataset with 29 films, followed by Screen Australia with 21. In contrast, Australian Film Finance Corporation appears in just nine films, highlighting how strongly film representation is concentrated among a handful of organisations.

Yet quantity does not tell the whole story. While larger rectangles represent more films, darker shades reveal higher median audience ratings, offering a different perspective on each company's contribution.

These differences raise an important question: Does greater output necessarily translate into better audience reception?

Number of Films by Country

Film counts represented in the dataset vary substantially between countries.

A Small Player in a Global Industry

Cinema is a global industry, but film production is far from evenly distributed across the world.

The United States dominates the dataset with 17,004 films, far ahead of France (2,932) and the United Kingdom (2,894), which rank second and third respectively.

Against these global giants, Australia occupies a much smaller position, ranking 13th with just 470 films. Yet its relatively modest presence raises an important question: does producing fewer films necessarily mean having less influence on the international stage?

Film counts reveal only one side of the story. Audience reception and international collaborations may offer a different perspective on Australia's place in global cinema.

09

Average Film Score by Country

Average scores provide another way to compare national film landscapes.

Beyond Quantity: A Different Measure of Success

Producing more films may increase a country's global presence, but does it necessarily lead to better audience reception?

While the previous map revealed the dominance of major film-producing nations, audience ratings tell a different story. Across the world, variations in blue reveal a diverse landscape of audience reception, suggesting that production volume alone cannot explain how films are rated.

Australia, despite ranking 13th in film count, records an average audience score of 6.30, placing it 93rd globally in the dataset.

This contrast highlights an important distinction: a country's presence in global cinema does not necessarily reflect its audience ratings. Yet ratings are only one measure of success, and smaller national samples may produce less reliable comparisons.

10

How Australian Cinema Connects to the World

International co-productions connect Australia to a broader global production network.

Beyond Borders: Australia's Global Cinema Connections

Australia's place in global cinema is not defined by film production numbers or audience ratings alone. Beyond its domestic industry lies a network of international partnerships.

The United States stands as Australia's largest co-production partner, appearing alongside Australia in 183 films in the dataset. The United Kingdom follows with 85 films, while Canada ranks third with 23, revealing particularly strong connections with English-speaking film industries.

Yet Australia's collaborations extend far beyond these three countries, reaching across Europe, Asia and neighbouring New Zealand.

These connections reveal another dimension of Australian cinema: its participation in an international network of filmmaking.
