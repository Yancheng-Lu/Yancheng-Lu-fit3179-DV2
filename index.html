Australian Cinema in Motion

How has Australian cinema changed?

A Cinema in Motion

From a Quiet Beginning to a Growing Presence

Australian-linked cinema began with a modest presence in the dataset. Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, most years recorded fewer than five films, with occasional increases followed by quieter periods.

The early 2000s brought gradual growth, but 2010 marked a dramatic turning point. Film counts rose sharply, reaching an annual peak of 31 films in 2014, a striking contrast with earlier decades.

Yet growth remained uneven. Counts fluctuated through the late 2010s and into the 2020s, and never returned to the 2014 peak. The pattern suggests increasing representation rather than continuous growth.

This expanding but unpredictable landscape raises a question: were particular genres driving this growth, or did different types of films follow their own trajectories?

Genres Rise, Fall and Return

The Changing Faces of Australian Cinema

Australian cinema has never been defined by a single genre. Comedy ranked first throughout the 1980s and 1990s, before Action took the lead in the 2000s and Drama in the 2010s.

By the 2020s, Horror had climbed to first place, while Comedy fell to fifth. This striking reversal followed Horror's absence from the top five in the 1990s.

Drama remained remarkably consistent, placing in the top three across all five decades. Adventure held a top-five position until the 2000s, while Animation returned in third place in the 2020s. Documentary appeared briefly in the 2010s.

These shifts reveal an evolving landscape where no single genre remains permanently dominant.

Changing Genres, Changing Fortunes

More Films, Not Always Bigger Fortunes

A genre's prominence does not always translate into box-office success. Film counts and commercial performance can tell very different stories.

The 2000s saw strong median grosses: Animation led at $260 million, followed by Horror at $68.3 million and Comedy at $55 million. Action and Adventure recorded $51.4 million and $49.6 million respectively.

These fortunes shifted. In the 2010s, Adventure fell to $1.9 million and Horror to $611,000. By the 2020s, Animation reached only $1.2 million, while Comedy fell below $1 million.

Despite ranking first in film count in the 2020s, Horror's median gross was just $1.3 million, compared with $7 million for Action. Greater representation does not necessarily translate into stronger commercial returns.

Commercial Success

What does success look like?

From Era to Genre to Box Office

Many Films, Few Blockbusters

As Australian cinema evolved, its journey to box-office success followed very different paths. The 2010s contributed the largest flow of films with reported box-office earnings, while Action emerged as one of the most prominent individual genres.

Yet more films did not necessarily mean more blockbusters. Despite the diversity of genres and decades, the largest share of films flowed into the Under $10 million category.

Action films appeared across all box-office tiers, including the highest, but only a relatively small proportion of films reached $150 million or more.

This reveals a striking contrast: while Australian-linked cinema expanded its presence in the dataset, major commercial breakthroughs remained comparatively rare.

Blockbusters and Expensive Misses

Big Budgets, Bigger Risks

Spending millions does not guarantee making millions. Some Australian-linked films earned extraordinary returns, while others fell short of their reported production budgets.

The Matrix Reloaded recorded the largest positive gap at $588.6 million. The Lego Movie and The Matrix also exceeded their production budgets by over $400 million each.

The Adventures of Pluto Nash had the largest negative gap at $92.9 million, followed by Better Man at $87.5 million. Australia and Three Thousand Years of Longing fell short by $80.4 million and $39.7 million respectively.

These contrasts highlight the financial risks of filmmaking: bigger budgets can bring exceptional returns, but also substantial shortfalls.

Did Audiences Think the Films Got Better?

More Films, But Were They Better?

As Australian-linked cinema expanded, did audience ratings improve alongside it?

The 1990s emerged as the strongest decade for highly rated films, with 37.1% scoring 7 or above. This changed sharply in the 2000s, when the highly rated share fell to 12.5%, while low-rated films rose to 45.3%.

By the 2020s, the balance shifted again. Low-rated films declined to 20.8%, but 60% received moderate ratings. This suggests a move towards more consistently average reception rather than a rise in exceptional acclaim.

These changes show that more films do not necessarily mean better-rated films. Growth in the number of films represented in the dataset was accompanied by changing audience reception, rather than steady improvement.

The Companies Behind Australian Film

Behind the Screen: A Few Names, Many Films

Australian-linked cinema involves many studios and supporting organisations, but their presence in the dataset is uneven. A few major names account for a substantial share of the films represented.

Warner Bros. leads with 29 films, followed by Screen Australia with 21. Australian Film Finance Corporation appears in nine, while Kennedy Miller and See-Saw Films are associated with seven and six respectively.

Yet quantity does not tell the whole story. Larger bubbles represent more films; darker shades indicate higher median audience ratings. Kennedy Miller's smaller, darker bubble suggests that greater output does not necessarily mean stronger audience reception.

These differences distinguish production scale from audience reception: smaller contributors may be associated with higher-rated films than more prolific organisations.

Number of Films by Country

A Small Player in a Global Industry

Cinema spans nearly every continent, but film production is unevenly distributed. The number of films represented in the dataset varies dramatically between countries, revealing a concentrated global landscape.

The United States leads with 17,004 films, far ahead of France with 2,932 and the United Kingdom with 2,894, ranking second and third respectively. These counts highlight the strong representation of North American and major European film industries.

Against these larger industries, Australia ranks 13th with 470 films. Its recorded output is much smaller, yet it remains part of an international network of filmmaking.

This raises a question: does producing fewer films necessarily mean less international influence? Film counts capture only one dimension; audience reception and co-production partnerships offer other perspectives on Australia's place in global cinema.

Average Film Score by Country

Beyond Quantity: A Different Measure of Success

Producing more films may increase a country's global presence, but does it lead to better audience reception?

While the previous map showed major producers dominating film counts, the varying shades of blue reveal a different pattern of ratings. Production volume alone cannot explain audience scores.

Australia ranks 13th in film count, yet its average audience score of 6.30 places it 93rd globally in the dataset.

This contrast separates production scale from audience reception. Ratings offer only one measure of success, and smaller national samples may make comparisons less reliable.

How Australian Cinema Connects to the World

Beyond Borders: Australia's Global Cinema Connections

Australia's role in global cinema extends beyond film counts and ratings to a network of international partnerships.

The United States is its largest co-production partner, sharing 183 films in the dataset. The United Kingdom follows with 85 films, while Canada ranks third with 23.

These links show strong ties with English-speaking film industries, alongside collaborations across Europe, Asia and neighbouring New Zealand.

Such connections highlight Australia's participation in an international filmmaking network, adding another perspective on its global presence.
